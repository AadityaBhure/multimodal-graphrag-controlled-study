# Part 4 (RQ4) · The integrated system

## The question and the design

**Research question.** What is the combined effect of the empirically justified choices from Parts 1 to 3 when they run together in one system?

The design (decision PR-11) was frozen before any RQ2 or RQ3 outcome was analysed. It defines one integrated system, **INT-1**, whose components are chosen mechanically by rules written in advance.

Table: RQ4 design {w=1.6,4.6}
| Element | Setting |
|---|---|
| System | INT-1 = selected retrieval-control policy → CM-B1 on graph evidence → selected fusion method → that method's answer |
| Context management | Fixed to **CM-B1** in advance, for cost: CM-01 was the quality-oriented candidate from Part 1 but is about 3.5 times as expensive; it is reported as not integrated |
| Rule R-AR | Among the RQ3 Pareto set (answer F1 and source F1 higher; tokens and latency lower), take the policy with the highest answer F1; ties go to fewer tokens, then the order AR-B1, AR-01, AR-02, AR-03, AR-B0 |
| Rule R-CF | Among the RQ2 Pareto set (answer F1 higher; input tokens and latency lower), take the method with the highest answer F1; ties go to fewer tokens, then the order CF-B1, CF-01, CF-02, CF-03, CF-B0 |
| Cohort | The 28 questions of Part 3 (14 families × normal and noisy) |
| Comparisons | INT-1 against AR-B0 (always search everything) and against the selected policy on its own, both already recorded in Part 3; family-clustered paired tests with Holm over the 2 pairs |
| Runs | 28 INT-1 records; 0 errors. The policy stage reuses the stored responses of Part 3, so identical requests are answered byte for byte from the store. |

The integrated system is therefore **AR-B1 → CM-B1 → CF-B1**. When the overall ranking of Chapter 18 was later defined, its three winners turned out to be exactly these three components, so INT-1 is both the pre-registered choice and the combination of the overall winners.

## How INT-1 works

@figure figures/m_int_1.png | INT-1: the rule router retrieves; CM-B1 trims the graph items; CF-B1 writes the three sections; the answer call produces the final answer. | 6.6

1. **Retrieval control (AR-B1).** The rule router chooses the sources and queries each once, exactly as in Part 3. Its evidence memory (text, graph and structured items) becomes the system's evidence.
2. **Context management (CM-B1).** Applied to the graph items only, at item level: keep the items of the five best-ranked papers (in retriever order) with their paths. Text and structured items pass through unchanged.
3. **Fusion (CF-B1).** Write the text, trimmed graph and structured items as three labelled sections within the 24,000-character budget, exactly as in Part 2.
4. **Answer.** The fusion method's answer call (shared prompt, shared model) produces the final answer. It replaces the policy's own answer.

Because the frozen graph retriever already returns at most five papers per search and AR-B1 calls it at most once, CM-B1 had nothing to remove in this study: it is a no-op for this single-graph-call policy, which is stated as a limitation.

## Findings

1. **The integrated system joins the three overall winners.** The rule fixed before the results ({{RQ4.sel.R-AR}} for retrieval control, {{RQ4.sel.CM}} for context, {{RQ4.sel.R-CF}} for fusion) and the overall ranking defined afterwards agree.
2. **It gives the same answers as both comparisons** (answer F1 {{RQ4.INT-1.answer_f1}}; every difference is exactly 0).
3. **It keeps the router's better source selection,** significantly better than searching everything ({{RQ4.test.source_f1.INT-1~AR-B0.delta}} source-selection F1, Holm p = {{RQ4.test.source_f1.INT-1~AR-B0.p_holm}}).

## Why the simplest strategies won

1. **Answers did not separate the strategies.** In Part 1 no difference was significant (and none could be), and in Parts 2 to 4 every strategy returned identical answers. With quality tied or untestable, cost and evidence selection decide the ranking.
2. **The elaborate methods add cost where the bottleneck is not.** Rerankers, Steiner trees, diffusion, entity alignment, model-based selectors, per-source reasoning, trained routers, calibrated evaluators and agents all act *after* retrieval. When the retrievers do not return the correct papers, better handling of what they did return cannot recover them.
3. **The simple controls are well matched to clean, template-like questions.** The rule router benefits from cue words in the question templates, and the fixed top-K matches the retriever's own ranking. On more natural questions this advantage may shrink (Chapter 32).

## Why answer F1 is low, and identical in Parts 2 to 4

@figure figures/c_upstream.png | Where answer quality is lost. Left: in Part 1, an average F1 of 0.250 comes from abstaining correctly on the unanswerable quarter of the questions; the coloured part is the contribution of real answers. Middle: at most 0.338 of the correct papers reach the context in Part 1. Right: in Part 3, only structured-only (and, weakly, graph-only) questions are ever answered. | 6.6

The low and identical scores have one cause: **the correct papers rarely reach the model.**

- **The text retriever cannot find exact-phrase conditions.** In the retrieval-only closeout B1's gold coverage is effectively zero; text conditions are exact phrases, while B1 searches by meaning (Chapter 13).
- **The graph retriever's top-5 is far smaller than the answer sets.** On graph-only questions every returned paper is correct, but five papers out of, for example, 78 correct ones give a recall near 0.04.
- **Only structured-only questions are answered exactly.** The structured retriever returns the exact answer, so every policy and every fusion of it scores 1.0 there.
- **Part 1's floor is the unanswerable variants.** A quarter of its questions have an empty correct answer, and all methods abstain on them, which gives 0.250; on the 18 answerable multi-source questions no method returned a correct paper.
- **Part 2's bundles rarely contain the answer:** {{RQ2.support.UNSUPPORTABLE}} of 24 contain no correct paper; on the 2 fully supportable bundles (both conflict variants) the model abstained.
- **The same evidence gives the same answer.** In Parts 2 to 4 the strategies changed how the same evidence was presented or which sources were searched, not which correct papers the retrievers could return, so the answers coincided.

## What the results do and do not show

**They show,** on this benchmark and cohort, that (1) none of the nine literature-inspired methods improved answer quality over simple controls with statistical support; (2) the rule router chooses sources significantly better and more cheaply than searching everything; (3) the trained router and the agent cost significantly more tokens and time for the same answers; (4) the integrated system inherits the router's source selection at a modest token cost; and (5) the binding constraint is upstream retrieval coverage.

**They do not show** that the elaborate methods are useless in general (their benefit may need better retrieval, longer contexts, a stronger model or more natural questions), that the controls are best on other benchmarks, or anything about the locked test set. The cohorts are small and their labels are verified by computation rather than by people.
